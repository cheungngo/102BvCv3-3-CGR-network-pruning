# **Network Pruning of a 12-Component Oral Glutamatergic Regimen: Leave-One-Out Ablation, Pharmacokinetic Sensitivity, and a ChEMBL Replacement Screen**

## **Abstract**

A multicomponent oral glutamatergic regimen could, in principle, influence several processes involved in synaptic plasticity. Testing all 12 components together, however, would make it difficult to attribute benefit or harm and would increase the burden of evaluating drug interactions. We assess the marginal contribution of each component of the proposed Cheung Glutamatergic Regimen (CGR). The analysis integrated human ChEMBL bioactivity at pChEMBL ≥ 5.5, Open Targets association scores for major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, and obsessive–compulsive disorder, and an undirected STRING graph containing 145 proteins and 904 edges. We compared three leave-one-out models, including one that applied a pharmacokinetic penalty to dextromethorphan when bupropion was removed. We also ran 200 degree-bin-matched randomizations per component and screened ChEMBL for phase-4-coded alternatives.

Dextromethorphan, bupropion, piracetam, and *Sceletium* ranked highest in the pharmacokinetic model. Bupropion’s mean marginal coverage loss increased from 0.00002 to 0.03309 when the dextromethorphan penalty was applied. Piracetam and *Sceletium* exceeded their respective null expectations at nominal significance, but neither passed a 5% false-discovery-rate correction across 12 tests. Fisetin had the largest shortfall relative to its null mean. Of 33 ChEMBL records mapped to at least two screening targets, only dextromethorphan and dextromethorphan hydrobromide met the phase criterion. Both represent the same active moiety. The four highest-ranked components, termed CGR-Lite, warrant joint computational testing. The present analysis does not establish that they preserve full-stack coverage, act synergistically, or constitute a safe or effective treatment.

## **1\. Introduction**

### **The Clinical Bottleneck in Psychiatric Therapeutics**

Persistent symptoms are a problem across psychiatric diagnoses, but the nature of treatment resistance differs. In major depressive disorder (MDD), treatment-resistant depression (TRD) is commonly defined for research as failure to remit after at least two adequate antidepressant trials. Diagnostic accuracy, treatment duration, and adherence matter because an inadequate trial can be mistaken for biological resistance (Sforzini et al., 2022). In bipolar disorder, treatment decisions must also account for the risk of mood activation. In schizophrenia, negative symptoms and cognitive impairment can persist despite improvement in positive symptoms. In obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), substantial symptoms can remain despite serotonin reuptake inhibition and evidence-based psychological treatment. Genetic studies point to several biological pathways within and across these disorders, but they do not establish a common mechanism of nonresponse or identify a drug combination likely to work across diagnoses (Howard et al., 2019; Mullins et al., 2021; Strom et al., 2025; Trubetskoy et al., 2022).

Ketamine provided clinical evidence that meaningful antidepressant effects can occur through pharmacology distinct from that of conventional monoaminergic antidepressants (Berman et al., 2000). Experimental work has linked glutamatergic perturbation to AMPA receptor signaling, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), its receptor TrkB, mTOR signaling, and changes in dendritic spines (Duman & Aghajanian, 2012; Harward et al., 2016; Li et al., 2010). These findings motivate a distinction between **initiating** a plasticity response and **maintaining** it. They do not show that durable clinical improvement requires every step in a fixed sequence, or that an oral combination can reproduce ketamine’s effects. That distinction is central to interpreting a network analysis: the model can locate components near proteins involved in these processes, but it cannot observe the processes themselves.

### **The Promise and Practical Limits of Multi-Component Regimens**

Dextromethorphan–bupropion, developed as AXS-05 and marketed as Auvelity, establishes a clinical precedent for oral dextromethorphan exposure supported by CYP2D6 inhibition. In adults with MDD, the GEMINI trial found improvement in depression scores relative to placebo, with separation evident at week 1\. A separate randomized trial compared the combination with bupropion alone (Iosifescu et al., 2022; Tabuteau et al., 2022). These trials support the efficacy of the tested formulation in MDD. They do not identify the relative contributions of NMDA receptor antagonism, sigma-1 receptor agonism, or serotonin transporter inhibition to the clinical effect. Nor do they validate the addition of piracetam, botanicals, or metabolic precursors (U.S. Food and Drug Administration \[FDA\], 2026).

Cheung’s earlier hypothesis proposed dextromethorphan, a CYP2D6-inhibiting antidepressant, piracetam, and glutamine (Cheung, 2026c). The subsequent staged framework narrowed the investigational core to dextromethorphan, one prespecified CYP2D6 inhibitor, and piracetam. It made glutamine optional, placed nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) and N-acetylcysteine (NAC) in a separate augmentation stage, and deferred senescence-directed agents until after initial pharmacokinetic and safety studies. The framework was written for unipolar TRD, reported no original clinical or laboratory data, and did not include *Sceletium* in its core (Cheung, 2026e). Related publications on Alzheimer’s disease prevention, autism, and healthspan provide context for the broader hypotheses, not independent validation of the 12-component stack analyzed here (Cheung, 2026a, 2026b, 2026d).

Testing 12 agents together would complicate both the study and its interpretation. Each addition raises questions about formulation, exposure, adherence, interactions, and attribution of adverse events. Reducing the number of candidates could make early studies easier to interpret, but only if the reduction is tested rather than assumed to preserve the properties of the full stack.

### **Study Objectives**

The first objective was to identify which components made the largest marginal contributions to a disorder-weighted network score and determine whether their rankings changed after curated pathway assignments or a pharmacokinetic interaction were introduced. We refer to the four highest-ranked components in the pharmacokinetic model as CGR-Lite. The term denotes a candidate subset, not a demonstrated minimum. A component can contribute little when removed from a large stack yet become important once several overlapping components are removed. The converse is also possible: the highest-ranked components may overlap with one another when scored as a smaller subset.

The second objective was to identify candidates for omission in subsequent analyses. A low marginal score could reflect overlap with another component, a poor fit to the synaptic panel, sparse assay data, or a mechanism that the graph does not represent. The third objective was to compare association profiles across MDD, bipolar disorder (BIP), schizophrenia (SCZ), and OCD and use the differences to frame testable hypotheses, without treating diagnostic association scores as predictors of individual response. The fourth was to ask whether the ChEMBL screen identified a distinct phase-4-coded active moiety spanning several targets in the panel. None of these objectives required, or permitted, an inference about clinical efficacy from network coverage alone.

## **2\. Methods**

### **Versioned Synaptic Plasticity Backbone & Disorder Weighting**

The script embedded a 121-gene Synapse Ledger. It comprised 106 backbone genes assigned to one or more of four modules—`LTP_init`, `TrkB_mTOR`, `Actin_spine`, and `Adhesion_PSD`—and 15 pharmacological anchors not in the backbone. The module labels describe the panel’s organization; they were not separate terms in the coverage calculation. The use of curated synaptic annotations is consistent with the approach taken by SynGO, but the 121-gene ledger was an analysis-specific panel, not a SynGO export or an independently validated disease module (Koopmans et al., 2019).

The script queried the Open Targets GraphQL `associatedTargets` endpoint for MDD (`MONDO_0002009`), BIP (`MONDO_0004985`), SCZ (`MONDO_0005090`), and OCD (`MONDO_0008114`). It requested the first page of up to 3,000 rows per disorder, matched approved gene symbols to the ledger, and assigned zero to genes absent from the returned rows. A zero thus denotes no score used by this implementation, not evidence that a gene has no disease association. The query retrieved the aggregate `score` field without restriction to genetic evidence. The resulting weights can reflect heterogeneous evidence, including evidence relevant to known drug targets; they are not GWAS effect sizes, causal probabilities, or independent genetic validation of the screen (Open Targets, n.d.).

### **Compound–Target Profiling & Curated Mechanistic Mapping**

The 12 entries were dextromethorphan, piracetam, bupropion, *Bacopa* represented by a bacoside A query, forskolin, *Sceletium* represented by a mesembrine query, citicoline, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), fisetin, L-glutamine, NAC, and NMN. The script attempted an exact ChEMBL preferred-name match before a fuzzy search, with additional aliases for selected compounds. When available, it used the returned parent molecule identifier. This process reduces some naming mismatches but does not establish that the selected record represents every constituent of a botanical preparation.

The component profiles retained human assay records with reported pChEMBL ≥ 5.5 and a standard activity type of Ki, Kd, IC50, or EC50. ChEMBL defines pChEMBL as the negative base-10 logarithm of eligible standardized molar activity values. These endpoints are broadly comparable on a log scale, but they measure different things (EMBL-EBI, n.d.; Hunter et al., 2025). The code did not filter on ChEMBL assay type or assay-to-target confidence score. Accordingly, `binding_only` is a legacy label: that mode uses database-derived bioactivity and is not restricted to direct binding assays. The script took the median reported value for each ChEMBL target record, expanded mapped complexes to their constituent gene symbols, and retained the highest resulting value per gene. An assay of a heteromeric receptor could therefore contribute the same value to more than one subunit. This is a mapping convention, not independent evidence of binding to each subunit.

The `CURATED_MECHANISTIC_TARGETS` dictionary separately encoded proposed receptor, enzyme, and pathway relationships. These included AMPA subunits for piracetam, PDE4 isoforms and `SLC6A4` for *Sceletium*, and pathway neighbors for metabolic or structural components. Curated entries were assigned a default pseudo-pChEMBL of 6.0, with a sensitivity grid of 5.5, 6.0, and 6.5. If a gene had both a curated and a database-derived value, the model used the higher one. The pseudo-values represent modeling assumptions, not assay results or estimates of clinical potency.

### **STRING PPI Network, Occupancy-Scaled Proximity & PK Gating**

The human STRING query used the union of ledger genes, mapped bioactivity targets, and curated targets, with a required association score of at least 700\. The returned edges formed an undirected graph. The coverage calculation used unweighted shortest paths: STRING scores determined which associations entered the graph but did not change individual edge lengths. STRING associations need not indicate direct physical contact or establish the direction of a biological effect (Szklarczyk et al., 2023). The use of local paths and degree-aware comparison draws on Guney et al. (2016), but the present measure differs from their drug-to-disease distance. Here, each disorder anchor is scored against its best reachable component target.

For each reachable anchor–target pair, the model divided shortest-path distance by the larger of 1 and the target pChEMBL minus 4.5. It then calculated an occupancy-like weight: 1 divided by the sum of 1 and 10 raised to the power of 4.5 minus the target pChEMBL. The pair score was that weight divided by 1 plus the effective distance. The highest pair score was retained for each anchor. Coverage was the Open Targets–weighted mean of these anchor scores, limited to positively weighted anchors in the graph with at least one reachable target. The denominator could therefore change if ablation left an anchor without a reachable target. At distance zero, the distance term drops out but the occupancy-like weight remains, so model affinity still affects a direct hit. The midpoint of 4.5 is a scoring parameter, not a measured human receptor-occupancy threshold.

The three modes were `binding_only`, `with_metabolic` (which added all curated mechanistic mappings, not only metabolic ones), and `with_metabolic_and_pk`. In the last mode, removal of bupropion also reduced each dextromethorphan edge value by 1.0 before targets were pooled. This penalty stands in for the effect of losing CYP2D6 inhibition. It is not a measured change in affinity, a fitted exposure ratio, or a model of dextrorphan. Marginal loss was full-stack coverage minus coverage after removing one component, averaged equally across the four disorders.

### **Degree-Matched Permutation Null & Multiple Testing**

Each component’s observed marginal contribution was compared with 200 random target sets generated with seed 1234\. Only targets present in the graph counted toward the randomized set size. The script divided graph nodes into 10 bins by degree rank and reassigned each target’s scoring value to a node drawn without replacement within the corresponding bin, using the broader graph if the bin was exhausted. Disorder weights and the rest of the stack stayed fixed. For bupropion in PK mode, a random set restored the unattenuated dextromethorphan values only if it contained `CYP2D6`. This rule tests the model as specified; it does not independently establish the pharmacokinetic interaction.

The z-score standardized the observed delta relative to its component-specific null distribution. The empirical upper-tail p-value was one plus the number of draws at least as large as observed, divided by 201 (Phipson & Smyth, 2010). Positive z-scores and p ≤ .05 were treated as nominal evidence of above-null contribution. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was used to assess those findings in the context of the 12-component testing family (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995).

### **Phase-4 Single-Molecule Replacement Screen & Reproducibility**

The screen selected 12 targets from the intersection of the curated-inclusive pooled target set and the ledger, excluding `CYP2D6`. Targets were ranked by their mean Open Targets score across the four disorders, with the pooled pChEMBL or pseudo-pChEMBL as a tie-breaker. In order, the selected targets were `GRIN2A`, `SLC6A4`, `GRIN2B`, `GRIN1`, `GRIN2D`, `GRIN2C`, `GRIA1`, `GRIA3`, `PDE4B`, `GRIA4`, `GRIA2`, and `SIGMAR1`. The script reused mapped single-protein ChEMBL identifiers where available and otherwise searched for a human single-protein record. It queried `mechanism.json` and the first page of `activity.json` sorted by descending pChEMBL, with an activity cutoff of 6.5. Mechanism-only entries could qualify without a measured pChEMBL; the code assigned them 6.5 for scoring. Records had to map to at least two panel targets and meet `max_phase >= 4.0`. ChEMBL phase coding is not a substitute for U.S. product-level verification through Drugs@FDA or the applicable OTC monograph (FDA, n.d.-a, n.d.-b; Hunter et al., 2025).

The supplied script and detailed summary dated November 25, 2026, are the primary sources for the methods and numerical results, respectively (*102BvCv3-3 CGR Network Pruning Colab*, n.d.; *CGR Network Pruning Detailed Run Summary*, 2026). The script names the per-mode ablation and permutation CSVs, sensitivity results, compound profiles, and `results_fda_replacements.csv`. The underlying CSVs and cached API responses were not supplied for this manuscript. Exact data releases and assay-level provenance therefore could not be independently reconstructed from the summary, and a rerun against live endpoints need not reproduce the same snapshot.

## **3\. Results**

### **Emergence of the 4-Component “CGR-Lite” Spine**

Five of 12 components yielded mapped ChEMBL bioactivity above the query threshold, producing 39 resolved component–gene pairs. The seven without mapped hits were piracetam, *Bacopa*, forskolin, *Sceletium*, citicoline, L-glutamine, and NAC. The mesembrine lookup did not resolve to a ChEMBL identifier in the reported run. These results describe the query and mapping rules; they do not establish a lack of bioactivity. The STRING graph contained 145 nodes and 904 edges, including 118 of the 121 ledger genes.

In `with_metabolic_and_pk`, removing dextromethorphan produced the largest mean coverage loss, 0.04330. The next three components were bupropion (0.03309), piracetam (0.02813), and *Sceletium* (0.01840). Dextromethorphan’s mean loss was 0.16068 in `binding_only`. Bupropion’s was 0.00005 in that mode and 0.00002 in `with_metabolic`, where it ranked 11th. Its rise to second depended on applying the penalty to dextromethorphan when bupropion was removed. Piracetam and *Sceletium* each had zero marginal loss in `binding_only`; their contributions in the expanded model came from curated mappings.

The pseudo-pChEMBL sweep was run in `with_metabolic`, not PK mode. Dextromethorphan, piracetam, *Sceletium*, and L-glutamine held ranks one through four, respectively, at every tested value. L-glutamine was fifth in the PK ranking. Forskolin also held its rank, at tenth in the sweep. *Bacopa* and bupropion each moved one place; NMN and DHA each spanned two places, as did fisetin; NAC spanned three. Thus, three members of the proposed CGR-Lite subset held their ranks across the static sensitivity grid. The analysis did not establish rank invariance of the four-component PK ranking or evaluate joint retention of coverage after all eight other components were removed.

### **Degree-Matched Permutation Significance & PK-Gate Recovery**

Piracetam had the largest positive z-score (3.788, p \= .0199; four graph targets), while *Sceletium* had the smallest empirical p-value (z \= 3.088, p \= .0050; four of five curated targets present in the graph). Both findings were nominal; neither survived Benjamini–Hochberg correction at a 5% false-discovery rate across 12 components. With 200 draws, the minimum attainable p-value was 1/201, or approximately .0050. Dextromethorphan was first by raw marginal loss but did not meet the nominal permutation threshold (z \= 2.073, p \= .0746; nine graph targets). The curated map includes overlapping `GRIN1`, `GRIN2A`, and `GRIN2B` assignments for NAC and a shared `SLC6A4` assignment for *Sceletium*. Such overlap could help explain dextromethorphan’s smaller marginal contribution in the expanded model, but the run did not isolate the effect of those edges. It did not report a `binding_only` permutation test, so there is no corresponding binding-only p-value for comparison.

Bupropion’s z-score changed from −0.474 (p \= .7612) without the PK penalty to 1.531 (p \= .2040) with it. The other components’ permutation results were unchanged across those modes: the penalty was applied only when bupropion was removed or its modeled function was tested against a random target set. The PK result shows that the score responds to an imposed exposure dependency. It does not establish independently that bupropion is indispensable or that the selected penalty is clinically calibrated.

### **Outer-Ring Stratification & Pruning Candidate Identification**

L-glutamine had the largest marginal loss outside the PK-ranked four (0.01404). Eleven of its 12 curated genes were present in the graph. Its null mean was 0.0093, and its observed result did not exceed the null at the nominal threshold (z \= 0.533, p \= .1940). The null already accounted for target count and approximate degree; the contribution of footprint size alone was not tested. *Bacopa* (0.00749; z \= 1.405, p \= .1144), NMN (0.00600; z \= 0.948, p \= .2139), and NAC (0.00492; z \= 1.412, p \= .1095) showed smaller marginal losses. The NMN profile had four graph targets, whereas its curated map listed three genes. DHA (0.00379; z \= −0.423), forskolin (0.00146; z \= −0.440), and citicoline (0.00001; z \= −0.269) contributed less under the scoring rule. These results do not distinguish an unimportant mechanism from one the graph measures poorly.

Fisetin produced a mean loss of 0.00459 versus a null mean of 0.0271. Its 15 initial ChEMBL target records mapped to 20 graph genes following target expansion. Fisetin had the most negative standardized result (z \= −1.568; upper-tail p \= 1.0000), making it the clearest candidate for an omission analysis based on the shortfall relative to its own null. The upper-tail test did not assess significant underperformance; it assessed whether fisetin contributed more than random sets. Fisetin was also not the component with the smallest raw marginal loss. Those distinctions matter if omission is based on coverage alone rather than on how unusually well a component’s target profile fits the graph.

### **Disorder-Specific Association Profiles**

Among the 121 ledger genes, 73 received a nonzero score in MDD, 71 in BIP, 65 in SCZ, and 53 in OCD. In MDD, the five highest-scoring anchors were `GRIN2A` (0.675), `SLC6A4` (0.637), `GRIN2B` (0.608), `GRIN1` (0.606), and `GRIN2D` (0.600). In OCD, they were `SLC6A4` (0.692), `GRIN2A` (0.446), `CACNA1C` (0.412), `GRM3` (0.394), and `GRIA4` (0.388). In SCZ, `SHANK3` (0.703) ranked first, followed by `GRIN2A` (0.642), `GRM3` (0.612), `CACNA1C` (0.551), and `NRXN1` (0.539). In BIP, the highest scores were for `CACNA1C` (0.597), `SLC6A4` (0.590), `GRIN2A` (0.584), `GRM3` (0.443), and `GRIN2B` (0.414). These are rankings within the selected ledger, not genome-wide rankings or evidence of a limiting synaptic mechanism in patients.

### **Phase-4 Single-Molecule Replacement Screen**

Of 33 ChEMBL records mapped to at least two screening targets, two met the phase criterion: dextromethorphan polistirex (`CHEMBL1201461`) and dextromethorphan hydrobromide (`CHEMBL1256818`). Both mapped to `GRIN2A` and `SIGMAR1` and had mean four-disorder coverage of 0.6019. Their individual scores were also identical: 0.5791 for MDD, 0.6218 for BIP, 0.5707 for SCZ, and 0.6362 for OCD. The two records represent the same active moiety, not two independent replacement drugs. No distinct replacement active moiety was identified under the implemented filters. The screen did not establish the absence of an approved drug capable of influencing the same pathways through targets or mechanisms outside those filters.

## **4\. Discussion**

### **Mechanistic Coherence of the 4-Component Spine**

**Initiation and PK ratio control.** Dextromethorphan is an uncompetitive NMDA receptor antagonist and sigma-1 receptor agonist, and it inhibits the human serotonin transporter in vitro (FDA, 2026; Rickli et al., 2018). The ChEMBL mappings place it near several NMDA-related anchors, but an expanded complex assay does not establish independent binding to every `GRIN` subunit. CYP2D6 converts dextromethorphan to dextrorphan by O-demethylation. Human studies show that CYP2D6 phenotype and quinidine inhibition materially change parent and metabolite exposure; the Auvelity label documents bupropion’s effect on dextromethorphan metabolism (Capon et al., 1996; FDA, 2026; Pope et al., 2004; Schadel et al., 1995). Dextrorphan is a more potent NMDA channel blocker in comparative assays, whereas parent dextromethorphan has stronger serotonin transporter inhibition in reported comparisons. The parent–metabolite shift should not be interpreted as a switch to an established therapeutic receptor balance or as evidence that dissociation will be avoided (Taylor et al., 2016). The model includes neither metabolite pharmacology nor bupropion’s independent antidepressant effect as a clinical outcome.

**AMPA amplification and cAMP gating.** The piracetam assignment covers `GRIA1` through `GRIA4`, all of which are already members of `LTP_init`. Structural work supports allosteric binding to GluA2 and GluA3 and describes weak modulation; it does not validate equivalent activity at all four subunits or demonstrate clinical AMPA engagement (Ahmed & Oswald, 2010). Preclinical ketamine experiments connect AMPA signaling and L-type calcium channels to activity-dependent BDNF release, but they do not establish piracetam-specific `CACNA1C` engagement or a clinical effect of adding piracetam (Lepack et al., 2015). The small piracetam trial in patients receiving electroconvulsive therapy addressed cognitive disturbance and did not establish antidepressant efficacy (Tang et al., 2002).

The *Sceletium* hypothesis concerns cAMP breakdown. In vitro, mesembrine showed pronounced serotonin transporter activity, while mesembrenone had activity at both the transporter and PDE4 (Harvey et al., 2011). This distinction matters because the model assigned a PDE4-family profile to an entry queried as mesembrine. Slower cAMP hydrolysis could change signaling through protein kinase A and CREB-dependent transcription. The ledger’s `PRKACA`, `PRKACB`, and `PRKACG` are PKA catalytic subunit genes; `CREBBP` encodes a coactivator, not CREB itself. Studies of late-phase long-term potentiation support a role for transcription, protein synthesis, and BDNF, but do not show that a *Sceletium* preparation engages all four PDE4 isoforms in humans or converts early potentiation into durable remodeling (Pang & Lu, 2004). The four components offer a sequence that can be tested, not a sequence observed in this study.

### **Translational Prioritization: Tackling Polypharmacy & Heterogeneity**

The results suggest a research order rather than a clinical deployment schedule. The first step is to compare the proposed CGR-Lite subset with the full stack and with smaller alternatives, including the three-component core in Cheung’s staged framework (Cheung, 2026e). The second is to test whether individual additions improve a defined outcome rather than add several at once. The third is to test omissions, starting with fisetin if the objective is to remove the component with the largest shortfall against its own degree-matched null. Reducing the candidate list from 12 to four could simplify a study, but neither pill burden nor cost, adherence, interaction frequency, or clinical outcomes were measured here.

**MDD and unipolar TRD.** The MDD anchor profile motivates a comparison between ionotropic initiation and later TrkB–mTOR or CREB-related effects. Dextromethorphan–bupropion provides clinical precedent in MDD, while COMET provides open-label follow-up rather than a controlled demonstration of sustained remission (Axsome Therapeutics, n.d.; Iosifescu et al., 2022; Tabuteau et al., 2022). *Bacopa* studies provide preclinical evidence of changes in BDNF and CREB phosphorylation and separate human evidence concerning cognitive outcomes (\[CDRI-08 mouse study\], 2015; Kongkeaw et al., 2014; Kwon et al., 2018). They do not establish direct engagement of `NTRK2`, `CREBBP`, or `CAMK2A`, or benefit when *Bacopa* is added to dextromethorphan–bupropion. Whether *Sceletium* or *Bacopa* contributes to response durability, including in patients with prominent anhedonia or cognitive symptoms, would require separate, controlled comparisons.

**OCD.** Cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuitry and the established role of serotonin reuptake inhibitors give a reason to assess serotonergic burden before considering any augmentation (Bloch et al., 2010; National Institute for Health and Care Excellence \[NICE\], 2005; Stein et al., 2019). An exploratory comparison could prioritize investigation of the AMPA hypothesis and omit *Sceletium* from the initial candidate arm. The `GRIA4` score does not predict piracetam response, and the model cannot show that dextromethorphan saturates SERT in patients. It also did not measure the coverage cost or reduction in clinical risk from removing *Sceletium* in an OCD-specific subset.

**Schizophrenia.** The `SHANK3` and `NRXN1` rankings, together with common and rare variant studies, motivate attention to postsynaptic scaffolding beyond ionotropic receptor coverage (Singh et al., 2022; Trubetskoy et al., 2022). L-glutamine is a substrate in the astrocyte–neuron glutamate–glutamine cycle. Its 12 curated genes span glutamine synthesis and breakdown, transport, and vesicular glutamate loading. That relationship does not establish that oral glutamine enhances neuronal glutamate replenishment in patients (Bak et al., 2006). NAC has a rationale in glutathione synthesis and cystine–glutamate exchange, and it has been studied separately in schizophrenia and early psychosis (Berk et al., 2008; Conus et al., 2018; Dean et al., 2011). The curated NAC–`GRIN` relationships are indirect. In MDD, an adjunctive trial did not meet its prespecified primary endpoint, and an updated meta-analysis reported heterogeneous results (Berk et al., 2014; Peng et al., 2024).

NMN offers a way to test NAD-related target engagement. Human studies have reported changes in blood NAD measures; rodent studies have examined behavioral and bioenergetic effects. Neither establishes brain engagement or direct binding to `NAMPT`, `SIRT1`, or `PPARGC1A` in patients (Igarashi et al., 2022; Okabe et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2020; Yoshino et al., 2018). DHA offers a separate hypothesis involving membrane composition and lipid signaling (Bazinet & Layé, 2014). These are reasons to study L-glutamine, NAC, NMN, and DHA individually, not evidence that schizophrenia’s negative and cognitive symptoms reflect a single metabolic deficit or that adding all four to CGR-Lite would improve outcomes.

**Bipolar depression.** The `CACNA1C` ranking motivates questions about L-type calcium channels and glutamatergic excitability, not a prediction of response to a dextromethorphan–piracetam combination. Mood activation would require a separate development pathway. Any future exploratory protocol would need independent review of eligibility, background mood stabilization, and stopping rules for activation or mania; the presence of a mood stabilizer does not establish safety. Cheung’s staged TRD framework explicitly excluded bipolar depression from its primary population (Cheung, 2026e; FDA, 2026; Mullins et al., 2021; Yatham et al., 2018).

**Fisetin and graph blind spots.** Fisetin has a preclinical senotherapeutic rationale that is distinct from its score against a synaptic panel (Yousefzadeh et al., 2018). The present result does not establish tau clearance, clinically important kinase or cholinergic interactions, or a geriatric or neuroinflammatory indication. Conversely, low graph scores do not prove biological redundancy. Forskolin stimulates adenylyl cyclase, whereas PDE4 inhibition slows cAMP breakdown. The mechanisms can be complementary, but synergy requires direct comparison against an additivity model (Seamon et al., 1981). Likewise, DHA-related membrane processes and citicoline-related phosphatidylcholine synthesis involve properties such as bilayer composition and receptor trafficking that shortest paths do not measure. The `PLA2G4` assignments for DHA and `PCYT1A` and `CHKA` assignments for citicoline should be read as pathway mappings, not proof of direct binding or of peripheral graph position (Bazinet & Layé, 2014; Reactome, n.d.; Secades & Lorenzo, 2006).

### **Pharmacokinetic & Clinical Safety Guardrails**

A formal research protocol would need to specify one CYP2D6 inhibitor and formulation, reconcile concomitant medications, and characterize exposure to both dextromethorphan and dextrorphan. Genetic poor metabolizer status and inhibition-induced reduction in CYP2D6 activity are distinct sources of exposure variability. Paroxetine and fluoxetine are examples of strong inhibitors; duloxetine is a moderate inhibitor (Eli Lilly and Company, n.d.; FDA, 2026). Aripiprazole and risperidone, by contrast, are CYP2D6 substrates. Bupropion-mediated inhibition can change the exposure of concomitant substrates, so each drug would require label-specific review. The model does not justify a general dose adjustment or a rule for omitting bupropion.

The Auvelity prescribing information contraindicates MAOI coadministration and specifies washout requirements. It also identifies seizure disorder, current or prior bulimia or anorexia nervosa, and abrupt discontinuation of certain agents, including alcohol and benzodiazepines, as contraindications (FDA, 2026, §§ 2, 4, 5, 7, 8.8, 12). Combining dextromethorphan with *Sceletium* and background SSRIs or SNRIs raises a plausible serotonin-toxicity concern, but the additional risk from *Sceletium* has not been quantified. A study would need prespecified eligibility and stopping rules for serotonergic symptoms, elevated blood pressure, altered mental status, and mood activation, along with product-quality verification and specialist review. These are safeguards for investigating an unvalidated regimen, not instructions for using it outside a formal research protocol (Cheung, 2026e).

### **Methodological Limitations & Next Analytical Steps**

The main limitation is that the scoring model combines measured bioactivity with curated mechanistic and pathway edges. The strongest nominal permutation results came from two entries for which the run recovered no measured profiles. The pseudo-pChEMBL values, occupancy midpoint, heteromer expansion, maximum-across-targets rule, and dextromethorphan penalty all affect the ranking. The text generator’s printed coverage equation omits the occupancy term implemented in the code; the Methods above describe the implementation. A reproducible release should save the code revision, dependency versions, source releases, raw API responses, compound and target mappings, and activity and assay identifiers. It should preserve the link between the activity value used for scoring and its actual source record; the current gene-level export can retain the first target identifier even when a different record supplied the maximum value.

The null is conditional on a graph built from the selected anchors and component targets. Ledger genes accounted for 118 of 145 nodes, or about 81% of the randomization pool. That makes this principally a within-panel comparison; it does not establish that the p-values are conservative relative to a broader human interactome. Degree matching was approximate, and 200 draws gave limited resolution in the upper tail. Sensitivity analyses should use more draws, alternative matching rules, and an independently defined network. Comparison with a genetics-only weighting scheme would show how much the results depend on heterogeneous Open Targets evidence.

The most direct next step is to score CGR-Lite jointly against the full stack, the three-component core, and smaller subsets, using a fixed anchor denominator so that disconnected anchors cannot change the comparison set. Removing the shared NAC–`GRIN` and *Sceletium*–`SLC6A4` mappings separately and together would test the proposed buffering explanation. Directed or time-resolved models could then ask questions that undirected proximity cannot answer, including whether a combination changes the timing or magnitude of cAMP signaling. Finally, a stronger replacement screen would separate measured activity from mechanism-only links, verify target identities, reconcile formulations by active moiety, and confirm regulatory status and exposure separately. The present screen cannot establish brain exposure at the chosen threshold or explain why specific glutamatergic drugs failed in Phase 2 or 3 trials.

## **5\. Conclusion**

Dextromethorphan, bupropion, piracetam, and *Sceletium* ranked highest by leave-one-out marginal loss in the pharmacokinetic model. Three retained their ranks across the static pseudo-affinity sweep; bupropion’s high ranking depended on the imposed dextromethorphan penalty. Piracetam and *Sceletium* exceeded their component-specific nulls at nominal significance, but neither survived correction across the 12-component family. Fisetin had the largest shortfall against its own null mean and is a candidate for omission testing. The phase-4-coded ChEMBL screen returned two formulations of dextromethorphan and no distinct replacement active moiety under the implemented filters.

The immediate question is whether the proposed four-component subset retains enough coverage when evaluated together and whether its ranking persists under different target mappings, weighting schemes, and PK assumptions. Only then would it be reasonable to move from computational prioritization to staged research on exposure, safety, and target engagement, followed by controlled efficacy and durability testing if warranted. Disorder-specific associations can help formulate those questions. They cannot substitute for patient-level evidence, justify a universal regimen, or establish that any of the proposed combinations should be used in clinical care.
